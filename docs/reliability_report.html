What was tested, and how honestly

The rule: the engine never gets graded on data it was tuned with. The random-fill model is calibrated (trained) on published packing-physics anchors — sphere random close packing 0.64, M&M ellipsoids 0.685, rods at aspect ratio 8 → 0.467. Those rows are marked TRAIN below and shown only for transparency. The TEST rows are ground truth the engine has never seen: exact lattice mathematics, published part masses from supplier weight charts, and measured bulk densities from industrial vendors.

Reading the errors

The geometric engine is exact where exactness is checkable — it recovers perfect grid, hexagonal, and honeycomb lattices from raw CAD with zero error. The two mass cases bracket the modeling gap: reading a real STEP file under-weighs by ~5% (CAD models omit thread metal), and the parametric imperial nut over-weighs by ~8% (chamfers approximated). Counting is more accurate than weighing, and both are within quoting tolerance.

The random-fill model held up on data it never saw: poured steel shot within 1.7%, settled lead shot within 2.3%. That's the strongest evidence in this report, because those two numbers come from warehouse floors, not physics simulations.

Known limits — where the next accuracy lives

Layer nesting: layers stack flat; spheres and rounded parts that could settle into the hollows of the layer below (FCC-style) will beat the placed count by up to ~20%. The placed number is a guaranteed floor, never an over-promise. Random-fill for extreme shapes: the aspect-ratio curve is anchored at 7 shape points; very flat plates or tangly, hook-shaped parts (springs, clips) sit off the curve and deserve a physical pour test. Boxes are ideal: real cartons bulge, have liners, and lose corner volume — measure inside dimensions. And check weight before volume: a 9″ cube of steel nuts is ~125 lb; the box fails before the geometry does.
